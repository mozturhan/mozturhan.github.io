Research

I use psycholinguistic and neurolinguistic approaches to investigate language comprehension across the lifespan, focusing on adult and child first language (L1) speakers and adult second language (L2) learners, in order to understand whether successful sentence comprehension relies on qualitatively similar or different processing mechanisms across these groups, and to identify the factors that facili-tate language comprehension. To address these questions, I have developed expertise in a range of behavioral, psycholinguistic, and neurolinguistic methods, including electroencephalography (EEG), eye-tracking, self-paced reading, and judgment tasks. Applying these methods across adult L1 and L2 populations allows me to investigate whether successful comprehension relies on qualitatively similar or different processing mechanisms across groups, and to identify the factors that facilitate language comprehension. The first line of my research program examines the time course with which different cues are used to generate predictions among adult L1 speakers (Ozturhan et al., 2026). My dissertation examined verb prediction in Turkish, a context in which it has been proposed that prediction may not robustly occur. Using EEG, I addressed not only whether comprehenders predict upcoming verbs, but pre-cisely when different sources of information become available for doing so, namely argumenthood and argument role information about preverbal nouns. Results show that comprehenders use case morphology to distinguish arguments from non-arguments, and that only arguments guide verb pre-diction. In addition, these two types of information are used on different time courses, with argu-menthood available first and argument role recruited later. These findings indicate that the apparent failure of verb prediction is not an inability to predict, but a matter of timing because what matters is which information becomes available and how quickly.

I work with EEG/ERP, eye-tracking, self-paced reading, and judgment tasks, and analyze complex multi-source datasets in R (mixed-effects and Bayesian models) and Python.

Temporal dynamics of argument–verb computations in Turkish sentence processing

Dissertation · EEG/ERP · Adult L1-Turkish speakers · advised by R. Fiorentino & A. Gabriele

My dissertation examined verb prediction in Turkish, a context in which it has been proposed that prediction may not robustly occur. Results show that comprehenders use case morphology to distinguish arguments from non-arguments, and that only arguments guide verb prediction. In addition, these two types of information are used on different time courses, with argumenthood available first and argument role recruited later. These findings indicate that the apparent failure of verb prediction is not an inability to predict, but a matter of tim-ing because what matters is which information becomes available and how quickly.

Key finding: Cmprehenders rapidly restrict verb prediction to structural arguments, but argument-role information influences prediction only when additional time is available before the verb.

Examining the factors that impact feature reassembly: Subject-verb agreement in L1 and L2 Turkish

Speeded acceptability judgments · L1-Persian & L1-English L2-Turkish learners · revised and resubmitted, under review, Linguistic Approaches to Bilingualism

In Ozturhan et al. (revised and resubmitted, under review), we examined how the properties of a learner’s L1 influence the L2 acquisition of morphosyntax. In Turkish, 3rd person plural subject-verb agreement is optional and conditioned by the animacy of the subject, such that animate plurals can take both singular and plural-marked verbs, but inani-mate plurals are predominantly restricted to the singular verbs, making it a useful case for studying how learners acquire a feature that their L1 may encode differently. Working within the Feature Re-assembly framework (Lardiere, 2009), we compared L1-Persian and L1-English learners of Turkish on a speeded acceptability judgment task, addressing whether the detectability of the agreement pattern, conditioned by animacy, depends on the learner’s L1 and L2-Turkish proficiency. L1-Persian speakers, whose L1 ties 3rd person plural agreement to animacy but in a way that differs subtly from Turkish, showed persistent transfer from their L1, but L1-English speakers, whose L1 marks number agreement on the verb more categorically and does not encode animacy in this domain, started to show the target Turkish pattern as proficiency increased. This suggests that a feature could become easier to detect when the contrast between the two languages is salient in the input, rather than when the two systems resemble each other but differ in subtle ways.

Key finding: The findings add nuance to our under-standing of L1 transfer regarding feature reassembly, highlighting the importance of feature detectability and individual variation in proficiency level.

Linguistic and prgamatic factors facilitating children's comprehension of sentence meaning

Behavioral · Turkish & English-speaking preschoolers · reviewed, revisions in progress, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience

It has been observed that pre-school aged children make errors in comprehension of sentences involving the incorrect rejection of universally quantified sentences such as Is every child carrying a box? in situations where every child is carrying a box, but there is also an extra box that no one is carrying. My aim is understand whether children are sensitive to linguistic cues that structure discourse, and that their comprehension is facilitated when discourse is well organized, as indicated by focus marking in the first project and Question Under Discussion (QUD) manipulation in the second one. In one project with preschool-aged Turkish-speaking children, we examined the role of a linguistic factor, namely the focus-marking question particle in Turkish, on quantifier interpretation (Ozturhan & Minai, under review). When the focus-marking question particle was attached to the quantified subject, yielding a cleft-like interpretation such as It is every child that is carrying a box, children showed more adult-like responses and made fewer comprehension errors. These findings suggest that Turkish children can use this particle as a linguistic cue to determine sentence meaning during comprehension. I also examined whether children’s comprehension of sentences with universal quantifiers can also be facilitation by the pragmatic context, focusing on the role of the QUD manipulation in both Turkish (Ozturhan & Minai, under review) and English (Minai, Nobuki, & Ozturhan et al., 2025). To address this, we established a discourse context in which the target question was preceded by a question that directed attention to the number of individuals performing the relevant action, rather than to the extra object in the scene. When this quantificational QUD was clearly established in the experimental discourse, children in both Turkish and English showed more adult-like interpretation and made fewer comprehension errors.

Key finding: Children interpret quantified sentences more accurately when the discourse is well organized, whether through morphologically marked focus in Turkish or a QUD manipulation in Turkish and English.

Collaborative work: NSF multi-site ERP project

Since 2022 I have been a research assistant on Examining native language variability and its effects on second language processing (PIs: Alison Gabriele & Robert Fiorentino, NSF #1747143), an ERP investigation of referential dependencies in L1 and L2 speakers of English and Spanish. I coordinated EEG and behavioral data collection from 180 participants across labs in the United States and Spain, built MATLAB/EEGLAB preprocessing pipelines, integrated EEG data with a 12-task battery of verbal and cognitive measures, and trained research assistants in EEG methodology. For this project, we also individual differences in L2 processing, with the goal of identifying which skills and abilities support successful comprehension of long-distance dependencies in the L2 (Gabriele, Alemán Bañón, Ozturhan et al., 2026). In an ERP study examining referential ambiguity in L2 speakers’ L1 and L2, we tested the same participants in both languages (English and Spanish) and administered a broad battery of verbal and cognitive measures. The results revealed robust individual differences in both the L1 and the L2, with verbal abilities emerging as a particularly important pre-dictor of processing. These findings support models that assign a central role to lexical quality in sen-tence comprehension.
